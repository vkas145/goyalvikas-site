Building a Marketing Career in Indian B2B: What I Look for When Hiring

By Vikas Goyal  ·  August 2026  ·  8 min read

I have interviewed well over two hundred marketing candidates across my time at IndiaMART and Naukri, at levels ranging from fresh graduates to VP hires, and a clear pattern has emerged in who goes on to grow fast versus who plateaus two or three years in, regardless of where they started or what pedigree was on their resume. It has almost nothing to do with which tools they know or which campaigns they list as achievements. It has everything to do with three habits of mind that either show up early in a career or never fully develop.

The Resume Signals I Have Learned to Discount

Early in my hiring career, I over-weighted a few things that turned out to be poor predictors: brand-name companies on the resume, familiarity with a long list of tools, and polished campaign case studies with impressive-looking creative attached. All three are easy to accumulate without actually being good at the job. A candidate can spend three years at a well-known company running someone else's playbook, learn a dozen tools at a surface level, and produce a beautiful case study for a campaign that, on closer questioning, they did not actually design or measure. None of that predicts what happens when you hand them ambiguity.

The Three Things That Actually Predict Growth

1. Can they explain the "why," not just the "what"

In every interview, I push past "what did you do" into "why did you choose that approach over the alternatives, and what would you have done differently with more budget or more time." Candidates who plateau describe their work as a sequence of tasks. Candidates who grow fast describe it as a series of decisions, with reasoning, trade-offs, and an honest account of what did not work. I ask every candidate to walk me through one campaign that failed and what they changed because of it. The answer to that single question tells me more than the rest of the interview combined, because it reveals whether someone actually learns from outcomes or just moves on to the next task.

2. Do they reach for the number before reaching for the story

This is the Lean Six Sigma instinct in me, and I make no apology for testing for it directly. When I ask "how did you know the campaign worked," I want a number, a comparison, a baseline, not an anecdote about a happy client or a screenshot of high engagement. The candidates who instinctively reach for measurement, even imperfect measurement, over anecdote are the ones who scale their judgment as they get more responsibility, because judgment without a feedback loop does not improve, it just calcifies.

3. Do they take ownership past their formal scope

The clearest tell in a reference check, more reliable than almost anything said in the interview itself, is whether previous managers describe the candidate as someone who noticed a problem outside their lane and fixed it anyway, or someone who stayed carefully inside their job description. I have promoted people faster than their tenure would normally justify specifically because they fixed a broken lead-routing process that was not technically their job, simply because they noticed it was costing the company pipeline and nobody else had picked it up.

A specific bar I use for early-career hires: in a first-round interview, I ask every candidate to critique one live campaign of ours, using only what is publicly visible — our website, our ads, our content. Candidates who can identify two or three specific, defensible improvements within fifteen minutes, backed by reasoning rather than opinion, clear this bar. It is a small test, but in six years of using it, it has correlated more reliably with two-year performance than any other single interview question I run.

What Slows Careers Down, Even for Talented People

The most common pattern I see in marketers who plateau after two or three strong years is over-specialisation without a corresponding grasp of the full funnel. A performance marketing manager who can run brilliant paid campaigns but cannot explain what happens to those leads after handoff to sales has capped their own ceiling, because every senior marketing role in Indian B2B eventually requires cross-functional fluency, and that fluency is far easier to build early, while the stakes of not knowing are lower, than to retrofit later at a leadership level where the gaps become expensive and visible.

The second pattern is staying too long in a role that has stopped stretching them. Comfort is the quiet killer of a marketing career. I would rather hire someone who changed roles or companies every eighteen to twenty-four months for genuinely expanded scope than someone who stayed five years in a role that had stopped growing after year two, even if the latter's resume looks more stable on paper.

What I Tell People Early in Their Career

Get close to revenue as early as possible, even if it means a less glamorous role. A demand generation or growth role that sits close to the pipeline number will teach you more in eighteen months than three years of brand work that never has to answer for its business impact, not because brand work lacks value, but because proximity to revenue forces a discipline of measurement and accountability that is hard to build any other way. Learn to read a funnel and a cohort table before you learn a dozen tools, because tools change every eighteen months and the underlying logic of how a funnel behaves does not. And volunteer for the ugly, unowned problem in your organisation, the broken process nobody wants to fix, because that is consistently where I have seen the fastest career acceleration happen, mine included.

None of this is exotic advice. It is the same discipline that made Six Sigma useful to me two decades into a career that started nowhere near marketing: define the problem precisely, measure honestly, and let the data tell you what to do next rather than defending an instinct past the point the evidence stopped supporting it.
